Figure 6 summarizes the workflow: the DsPCBSD+ images are partitioned into training, validation and test subsets; the two detectors are fine-tuned and the Adaptive Resolution Router (ARR) is calibrated; and the trained models are then evaluated, individually and after Weighted Box Fusion (WBF), on the held-out test subset, before and after compression.

P[4] (Heading 3): 3.8.1 Dataset and Partitioning
P[5] (Normal): All experiments used the DsPCBSD+ benchmark [21] described in Section 3.1: 10,259 RGB images of 640 × 640 pixels with 20,376 annotated boxes across nine defect classes (SH, SP, SC, OP, MB, HB, CS, CFO and BMFO). The images were divided into training, validation and test subsets in an approximately 70% / 20% / 10% ratio (Table 5). The training subset was used to fit both detectors, the validation subset for checkpoint selection and for calibrating the ARR thresholds (Section 3.2.2), and the test subset was held out for final evaluation. P[19] (Heading 3): 3.8.4 Adaptive Resolution Router Configuration

P[33] (Heading 2): 3.9 Evaluation Metrics
P[34] (Normal): Two groups of metrics are used. Detection-quality metrics (IoU-based matching, precision, recall, F1-score and false-positive counts) assess correctness; efficiency metrics (latency, throughput, routing savings, model size, parameter count and compression ratio) assess computational cost. The notation below is used consistently in Sections 4 and 5.
P[35] (Normal): 
P[36] (Normal): Figure 7 | Detection evaluation protocol. Predictions are matched to ground-truth boxes by class and IoU; the resulting TP, FP and FN counts define precision, recall and F1-score. Efficiency metrics are computed separately.
P[37] (Heading 3): 3.9.1 Intersection over Union
P[38] (Normal): For a predicted box B_p and a ground-truth box B_g, the overlap is
P[39] (Normal): 	IoU(B_p, B_g) = |B_p ∩ B_g| / |B_p ∪ B_g|	(19)
P[40] (Normal): A prediction is a true positive (TP) when its class equals that of a still-unmatched ground-truth box and IoU ≥ τ_IoU; each ground-truth box can be matched once. An unmatched prediction is a false positive (FP) and an unmatched ground-truth box is a false negative (FN). [[TBD: value of τ_IoU used in evaluation; Section 3.6 uses 0.5 for fusion matching.]] IoU also serves as the matching criterion inside WBF. For PCB inspection, IoU ensures that a detection refers to the defect at the correct location on a trace or pad, which matters for repair and for separating adjacent defects.
P[41] (Heading 3): 3.9.2 Precision, Recall and F1-score
P[42] (Normal): 	P = TP / (TP + FP)	(20)
P[43] (Normal): 	R = TP / (TP + FN)	(21)
P[44] (Normal): 	F1 = 2PR / (P + R)	(22)
P[45] (Normal): Precision is the fraction of reported detections that are real defects; its complement, 1 − P, is the false-alarm burden that translates into manual re-inspection workload and the pseudo-defect problem of conventional AOI (Section 1.2). Recall is the fraction of real defects that are found; 1 − R is the miss rate, the critical quantity for FOD and for open or short circuits, where an escaped defect may cause a field failure (Section 1.1). F1, the harmonic mean of P and R, summarizes the balance at one operating point and penalizes a large gap between the two, which makes it suitable for comparing a high-recall with a high-precision detector. All three are reported at the confidence threshold [[TBD]] and are therefore operating-point dependent. [[TBD: micro- or macro-averaging over classes; add mAP@50 / mAP@50–95 here only if Section 4 reports them.]]
P[46] (Heading 3): 3.9.3 False Positives and False-Alarm Reduction
P[47] (Normal): The absolute false-positive count FP is reported for YOLO26n, RT-DETR-L and the fused output. The reduction achieved by fusion relative to detector d and the corresponding recall change are
P[48] (Normal): 	ΔFP_d = FP_d − FP_fused ,   ΔR_d = R_fused − R_d	(23)
P[49] (Normal): A positive ΔFP together with ΔR ≥ 0 indicates that fusion removes erroneous detections without discarding true ones. [[TBD: define whether the '17 false alarms' reported in the Abstract are box-level FP counts or image-level false alarms, and the split on which they were counted.]]
P[50] (Heading 3): 3.9.4 Accuracy
P[51] (Normal): Classification accuracy, (TP + TN)/(TP + TN + FP + FN), was not used. In object detection the true negatives (background regions correctly left undetected) are not well defined, so precision, recall and F1-score are used instead.
P[52] (Heading 3): 3.9.5 Latency and Throughput
P[53] (Normal): For N timed runs the mean per-image latency is
P[54] (Normal): 	T̄ = (1/N) Σ_{n=1..N} T_n	(24)
P[55] (Normal): where T_n is the wall-clock time for image n in milliseconds. T̄_YOLO, T̄_DETR, T̄_SR and T̄_ARR denote the latency of YOLO26n, RT-DETR-L, the super-resolution stage and the routing gate, respectively. Throughput at batch size 1 is
P[56] (Normal): 	FPS = 1000 / T̄	(25)
P[57] (Normal): Latency indicates whether a frame can be processed within the cycle time of the line, whereas throughput indicates the sustainable frame rate. Both depend on hardware and measurement protocol and are therefore reported together with the platform (Section 3.8.2). [[TBD: confirm whether throughput is reported in Section 4 and for which model and hardware.]]
P[58] (Heading 3): 3.9.6 Computational Savings of Adaptive Routing
P[59] (Normal): Let N be the number of images, N_SR the number routed to super-resolution and N_dir = N − N_SR the number processed directly. The fraction of super-resolution invocations avoided is
P[60] (Normal): 	S_route = N_dir / N = 1 − N_SR / N	(26)
P[61] (Normal): and the average per-image latency saving relative to blanket super-resolution is
P[62] (Normal): 	ΔT = T̄_all-SR − T̄_adaptive	(27)
P[63] (Normal): with the relative reduction in total processing time given by ΔT / T̄_all-SR. S_route measures computation avoided on frames that do not require enhancement, while ΔT also includes the cost of the gate. The per-class routing rate, SR% = N_SR,c / N_c (Table 4), relates routing decisions to defect scale through the median area ratio ρ. These metrics matter in AOI because super-resolution is the most expensive preprocessing stage and its cost scales with the number of enhanced frames.
P[64] (Heading 3): 3.9.7 Model Size, Parameter Count and Compression Ratio
P[65] (Normal): The parameter count |Θ| is the number of learnable weights and is a proxy for memory and computation demand. The model size M is the serialized file size in MB. The compression ratio and the pruning sparsity are
P[66] (Normal): 	CR = M_FP32 / M_INT8	(28)
P[67] (Normal): 	κ = (number of zero-valued weights) / |Θ|	(29)
P[68] (Normal): where M_FP32 is the ONNX FP32 file size and κ is the sparsity (30% target, Section 3.7.1; the symbol κ is used to avoid a clash with the area ratio ρ of Eq. 7). These quantities determine whether both detectors can be hosted together within the memory budget of an industrial PC or embedded accelerator (Section 2.4). Unstructured sparsity reduces the number of non-zero weights but does not by itself reduce the size of a dense file, so CR reflects quantization only.

P[74] (Heading 2): 5.1 Why YOLO26n Behaves Differently from RT-DETR-L
P[75] (Normal): The two detectors operate at different points of the precision–recall trade-off (Table D1, Figure D1a). YOLO26n is the more conservative detector, whereas RT-DETR-L recovers considerably more of the annotated defects at a markedly lower precision. Their F1-scores are close, which indicates that they differ not so much in overall balance as in where they place their errors: YOLO26n mainly errs by omission and RT-DETR-L mainly by over-detection. Relative to YOLO26n, RT-DETR-L finds about 32% more of the true defects (0.8663 / 0.6578) while about 22% fewer of its reported detections are correct (0.4924 / 0.6340).

P[80] (Normal): Local versus global representation. A convolutional detector builds each prediction from local receptive fields that are progressively downsampled. For the multi-scale defects of DsPCBSD+ (median area ratios from 0.003 for SP to 0.062 for CS, Table 2) the finest structures are represented by few feature-map cells after strided downsampling, a limitation described in Section 1.3. A transformer detector relates every encoded location to every other through attention and decodes a fixed set of object queries without NMS (Section 3.4). It is therefore reasonable to attribute the higher recall of RT-DETR-L to its ability to use surrounding context, such as trace continuity around an open circuit or a bridge, to propose candidates that local evidence alone would not support. This explanation is consistent with, but not directly verified by, the aggregate recall values. [[S4: if per-class recall is reported, state which classes show the largest recall gap; Table 4 identifies SP and MB as the most micro-scale classes.]]
P[81] (Normal): False positives. The lower precision of RT-DETR-L has two plausible sources. First, a query-based decoder emits a fixed number of predictions whose confidence is not constrained by NMS, so a given confidence threshold admits more low-evidence boxes. Second, contextual reasoning can propose defects where the surroundings merely resemble defect context, for example along irregular trace edges. Because precision and recall are measured at one confidence threshold, part of the observed gap reflects the operating point and the different confidence calibration of the two models, not only intrinsic capability. [[S4: if precision–recall curves or mAP are available, use them to separate these effects.]]
P[82] (Normal): Cost. RT-DETR-L has about 13 times as many parameters as YOLO26n (32.82 M versus 2.51 M) and about 7 times its GPU latency (28.0 ms versus 3.9 ms). Its recall advantage is therefore bought at a substantially higher computational price, which motivates its use as a second, complementary track rather than as a replacement (Section 5.3).
P[83] (Heading 2): 5.2 Why Fusion Helps
P[84] (Normal): The fused output keeps the recall of RT-DETR-L (0.8663), attains the highest F1-score of the three configurations (0.6493, versus 0.6457 for YOLO26n and 0.6279 for RT-DETR-L), and removes false positives present in the transformer output. Because the two detectors have almost equal F1-scores but very different precision–recall profiles, their errors are largely different, which is the condition under which ensembling is expected to help.
P[85] (Normal): 
P[86] (Normal): Figure D2 | Treatment of agreeing and isolated detections by WBF. Boxes found by both detectors are fused and reinforced; boxes found by only one detector keep a reduced confidence, so isolated low-evidence boxes are more likely to be removed by the output threshold while strong isolated boxes survive. This is the mechanism by which recall can be preserved while false positives are reduced.
P[87] (Normal): Mechanism. WBF does not discard overlapping boxes. It averages the coordinates of same-class boxes with IoU ≥ 0.5, weighted by w_i·c_i (Section 3.6), and, as described in [10], lowers the confidence of fused boxes that are supported by fewer models than the ensemble size. A detection proposed by both detectors is thus reinforced and its coordinates are refined by two independent estimates, whereas a detection proposed by only one detector keeps a reduced confidence and is more likely to fall below the output threshold. This is consistent with the observation that false positives of RT-DETR-L were removed without any loss of recall: true defects found only by RT-DETR-L must have retained sufficient confidence, while a subset of its isolated low-evidence boxes did not. [[S4: number of fused boxes supported by both detectors versus one detector, if reported.]]
P[88] (Normal): Asymmetric weighting. Setting w_DETR = 1.5 and w_YOLO = 1.0 gives the higher-recall detector more influence over box coordinates and confidence, so that its true detections are not suppressed when YOLO26n has no counterpart. The weighting was motivated by the validation recall of RT-DETR-L (Section 3.6). P[91] (Normal): Scope of the gain. The improvement should be read carefully. Relative to RT-DETR-L, fusion improves F1 by 0.0214 and improves precision. Relative to YOLO26n, the F1 gain is small (+0.0036) while the recall gain is large (+0.2085). The principal benefit of the dual-track system is therefore not a higher F1-score than the lightweight detector, but the ability to keep the transformer's recall at a higher precision than the transformer alone. The fused precision is not stated in the present manuscript; from F1 = 0.6493 and R = 0.8663 it follows that it is approximately 0.519 (derived). Claims about localization quality (tighter boxes from coordinate averaging) are a plausible expectation of WBF, but they are supported only if Section 4 reports IoU-sensitive metrics. P[92] (Heading 2): 5.3 Accuracy vs. Computational Cost
P[93] (Normal): Detector cost. RT-DETR-L dominates the cost of the dual-track engine: at 28.0 ms against 3.9 ms for YOLO26n, it accounts for about 88% of the combined 31.9 ms if the detectors run sequentially (Figure D3b). If they run concurrently, as in Figure 1, the detector stage is bounded by the slower track (about 28 ms) plus the WBF cost. [[S4/TBD: measured WBF time per image and whether concurrent execution was benchmarked.]] Relative to RT-DETR-L alone, adding YOLO26n and WBF costs a small increment, and returns a higher F1-score and fewer false positives at identical recall. Relative to YOLO26n alone, the fused system costs roughly seven to eight times the detector time in return for about 21 percentage points of additional recall. Whether this is justified depends on the cost of an escaped defect against the cost of extra computation: for FOD and open or short circuits, where a miss can cause field failure (Section 1.1), the recall gain is likely to be valuable, whereas for less critical defect types the YOLO26n track alone may suffice.

P[103] (Heading 2): 5.4 Deployment Implications
P[104] (Normal): 
P[105] (Normal): Figure D5 | Candidate deployment architecture. Frames pass through the ARR gate, are routed to the direct or super-resolution path, are standardized to 640 × 640, are processed by the two INT8 detectors and fused by WBF. The dashed boxes list issues that were not evaluated in this study.
P[106] (Normal): Latency and throughput. A 28.0 ms GPU latency for the slower track corresponds, as an upper bound that ignores all other stages, to about 35 frames per second (1000 / 28.0, derived). Frames routed to super-resolution incur additional preprocessing; Table 3 implies about 38.6 ms per image for blanket super-resolution (derived), so the per-frame latency depends on the route and a production line should be dimensioned for the super-resolution route and not for the mean. These estimates are indicative only and do not establish real-time performance on a specific line without end-to-end measurement. [[S4/TBD: end-to-end pipeline latency on GPU and CPU, and the target line rate.]]
P[107] (Normal): Memory and edge hardware. With INT8 sizes of 2.74 MB and 32.45 MB, the weights of both detectors fit comfortably within the 4–8 GB of VRAM of the edge platforms discussed in Section 2.4. Activation memory of the transformer and of the super-resolution model during inference was not characterized. [[TBD: peak GPU/CPU memory if measured.]] No embedded accelerator (for example, a Jetson-class device) was benchmarked in this study [[TBD: confirm]], so edge feasibility is supported by model size and CPU benchmarks, not demonstrated on target hardware.
P[108] (Normal): Reliability. The fused recall of 0.8663 implies that about 13% of annotated defects are still missed, and the implied precision of about 0.52 (derived) implies that about half of the reported detections are false alarms. In its present form the system is therefore better suited to a high-sensitivity screening stage with operator or secondary verification than to a fully autonomous accept/reject decision. The same applies to the FOD classes (CFO, BMFO), whose per-class recall should be checked against the line's escape-rate requirement. [[S4: per-class recall for CFO and BMFO.]]
P[109] (Normal): Practical configuration. Because the tracks are decoupled, an operator can select YOLO26n alone (lowest latency, higher precision), the full fusion (highest recall), or, as an option that was not evaluated here, the transformer only on frames where YOLO26n is uncertain. The ARR thresholds are explicit parameters that can be recalibrated to a line's illumination and board type.

Category | Assessment
Direct numerical comparison | Limited. The cited works report mAP@50 (90.4%–99.69%), whereas this study reports precision, recall and F1-score at an operating point. These quantities cannot be ranked against each other. The only study sharing the dataset, Ye et al. [1], used a different class set and evaluation protocol according to Table 1. [[S4: if mAP@50 is computed for the proposed system, add it as the only like-for-like number.]]
Dataset effects | Most cited studies use six-class datasets (DeepPCB, PKU-PCB, HRIPCB, custom sets). DsPCBSD+ contains nine classes including FOD, a 5:1 imbalance between the most (SP, 4,584) and least frequent (SH, 915) classes, and many defects below 0.5% of the image area (Table 2). Li et al. [2] report degraded accuracy on particulate debris, consistent with the difficulty of the FOD classes here. The same detector can therefore score much lower on such a benchmark.
Architectural differences | All studies in Table 1 use a single model with static preprocessing. Niaz et al. [20] and Selvam et al. [6] place transformer components inside one network, and Niu et al. [23] a single lightweight RT-DETR. The present framework differs by content-adaptive resolution handling, late fusion of two independent detector families (WBF [10]) and combined pruning and INT8 quantization. These are architectural distinctions, not accuracy claims.
Computational and deployment | Table 1 reports > 45 ms [6] and > 70 ms [20] for transformer-containing models, and pruning or distillation without INT8 in [1], [4] and [8]. The RT-DETR-L track (28.0 ms) and the INT8 sizes are in a comparable or lower range, but latencies were obtained on different hardware and cannot be ranked directly [[TBD: GPU of this work]]. The Table 1 entry 'Full INT8 quantization' should read 'dynamic INT8 post-training quantization', as in Section 3.7.2.
Qualitative differences | This study reports system-level properties (routing statistics, fusion behaviour, compression) that single-model studies do not address, notably the share of frames that can skip super-resolution. It does not show that these properties yield higher accuracy than the cited detectors. The absolute F1-score of 0.6493 should be regarded as a baseline on a nine-class benchmark.
