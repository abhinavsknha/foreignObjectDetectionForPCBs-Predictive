P[3] (Normal): Abstract
P[4] (Normal): Automated Optical Inspection (AOI) of High-Density Interconnect Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) faces a critical trade-off between inspection throughput and multi-scale defect detection fidelity. While lightweight convolutional neural networks (CNNs) offer high frame rates, they exhibit elevated miss rates on microscopic anomalies and Foreign Object Debris (FOD). Conversely, Vision Transformers provide global contextual awareness across intricate copper traces but impose prohibitive latency and memory overhead on industrial edge platforms. Furthermore, applying super-resolution (SR) universally across all frames creates severe computational bottlenecks. Here, we present an edge-deployable, high-throughput PCB defect detection framework featuring an Adaptive Resolution Router (ARR) that dynamically evaluates incoming frames via a multi-criteria perceptual complexity gate. Across the 10,259-image DsPCBSD+ benchmark, the router bypasses super-resolution for 55.13% of frames containing macro-defects while selectively enhancing the remaining 44.87% containing micro-defects, saving 22.1 ms per image (55.13% compute reduction). An asymmetric dual-track detector pairing YOLO26n (3.9 ms) and RT-DETR-L (28.0 ms) harmonized via Weighted Box Fusion achieves 86.63% recall and a peak F1-score of 0.6493, eliminating 17 false alarms with zero recall loss. Post-training INT8 quantization yields up to 3.87x compression, confirming real-time feasibility on embedded edge inspection hardware.
P[5] (Normal): Keywords: Printed circuit boards, Automated optical inspection, Surface defect detection, Foreign object debris, Vision transformers, Edge artificial intelligence.
P[6] (Normal): 1. Introduction
P[7] (Normal): 1.1 Industrial Context and the Quality Imperative in Electronics Manufacturing
P[8] (Normal): Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) serve as the fundamental structural and electrical backbone of virtually all modern electronic systems, spanning mission-critical aerospace avionics, autonomous automotive control units, biomedical implants, industrial telecommunications, and ubiquitous consumer electronics. With the aggressive scaling of high-density interconnect (HDI) substrates, multi-layer micro-via architectures, and ultra-fine-pitch surface-mount technologies (SMT), line widths and trace clearances have contracted to sub-100-micrometer tolerances [1, 2]. Under these sub-millimeter regimes, the structural and electrical integrity of PCB copper conductors, solder masks, and insulating substrate layers is exceedingly vulnerable to manufacturing variations and physical contamination.
P[9] (Normal): Surface defects occurring during fabrication (e.g., etching, plating, and solder masking) manifest in diverse morphological archetypes. These include severe electrical discontinuities such as open circuits (OP) and short circuits (SH); conductor degradation such as copper spurs (SP), spurious residual copper (SC), and fine conductor scratches (CS); and protective barrier failures such as solder mask breaks (MB) and hole breaks (HB) [2, 4]. Simultaneously, foreign particulate contamination—specifically metallic Foreign Object Debris (FOD), such as stray copper particles (CFO) and microscopic solder balls or ball-shaped micro foreign objects (BMFO)—poses an insidious operational risk. Foreign conductive particulate matter can migrate under operational thermal cycling and mechanical vibration, inducing intermittent shorting, arc faults, and catastrophic field failures long after post-production deployment. Consequently, continuous, high-speed, non-destructive Automated Optical Inspection (AOI) represents an indispensable quality control gate across smart electronics manufacturing lines (Industry 4.0) [1, 3].
P[10] (Normal): 1.2 Limitations of Conventional AOI and Early Deep Learning Systems
P[11] (Normal): Historically, industrial AOI systems have relied predominantly on rule-based machine vision pipelines and classical template matching [24]. These traditional approaches align incoming production images with an idealized golden board reference and compute defect candidate regions via structural differencing, morphological gradient operators, and hand-engineered color-space thresholding. While computationally modest, rule-based AOI systems exhibit profound fragility under real-world factory dynamics: (1) high pseudo-defect rates, where minor non-defective variations such as solder mask color shifts, non-uniform coaxial lighting gradients, micro-vibrations of conveyor belts, and negligible component placement tolerances are frequently flagged as anomalies, overwhelming human operators with false alarm rates often exceeding 20–30% [1, 6]; (2) inflexibility to product iterations, where any alteration to trace geometries or board layouts necessitates manual recalibration and golden template re-registration; and (3) inability to generalize across morphological variance, as deterministic geometric thresholds fail to accurately categorize stochastic defects, such as irregular particulate debris or subtle copper scratches exhibiting ill-defined boundaries.
P[12] (Normal): To transcend these limitations, deep learning algorithms—particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)—have been widely adopted for PCB defect localization [3, 5, 8]. Early applications utilized two-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN), which achieved respectable detection precision but suffered from high computational latency (>80 ms per frame), rendering them unsuitable for continuous multi-meter-per-second surface inspection lines. In response, single-stage object detectors from the You Only Look Once (YOLO) lineage (YOLOv5 through YOLOv11/v12) emerged as the dominant industrial paradigm due to their unified regression formulations and ultra-low inference latencies [1, 5, 6, 25].
P[13] (Normal): 1.3 The Scale Disparity Dilemma and the Super-Resolution Bottleneck
P[14] (Normal): Despite their operational speed, modern single-stage CNNs confront a severe performance degradation when applied to PCB inspection due to extreme scale disparity. As documented in modern industrial benchmarks such as DsPCBSD+ [2], defect surface areas exhibit dynamic ranges spanning multiple orders of magnitude:
P[15] (Normal): Macro-scale defects, such as hole breaks (HB) and large short circuits, occupy several thousand pixels (>= 2.5% to 6.0% of total image area), presenting distinct geometric contours that standard downsampling backbones capture readily. In stark contrast, micro-defects—such as ball-shaped micro foreign objects (BMFO), fine copper spurs (SP), and conductor scratches (CS)—often span fewer than 10 x 10 pixels, representing less than 0.5% of the total frame. When input images are downsampled to canonical detector resolutions (e.g., 640 x 640 pixels), the successive strided convolutions and spatial pooling operations of standard CNN backbones irreversibly attenuate the high-frequency spatial gradients of these microscopic anomalies. Consequently, standard CNNs exhibit high false negative rates (misses) on tiny defects.
P[16] (Normal): To mitigate this loss of feature resolution, prior researchers have explored two primary avenues: (1) dense slicing or tiling (e.g., Slicing Aided Hyper Inference - SAHI), which inflates inference latency linearly by a factor of N_tiles (often 4x to 16x); and (2) universal deep super-resolution (SR), which applies deep enhancement (e.g., SwinIR, Real-ESRGAN) to every image, adding 50–250 ms per frame. Crucially, existing literature applies super-resolution indiscriminately across all frames. In an industrial inspection batch, more than half of the processed boards contain either macro-defects or clean conductive traces where deep super-resolution yields no detectable accuracy gain, resulting in massive GPU compute wastage and thermal throttling on edge systems.
P[17] (Normal): 1.4 The CNN vs. Vision Transformer Duality in Defect Inspection
P[18] (Normal): Concurrently, the emergence of Vision Transformers (ViTs) and Detection Transformers (e.g., DETR, Deformable-DETR, RT-DETR) has offered a powerful alternative paradigm [20, 22, 28]. Unlike CNNs, which are constrained by local receptive fields, RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer) leverages multi-scale cross-attention mechanisms that model long-range spatial dependencies across complex conductive trace networks. This global context enables RT-DETR to differentiate true electrical track continuities from subtle opens (OP) and shorts (SH) with exceptional recall and fine-grained localization accuracy [23]. However, RT-DETR's transformer encoder-decoder layers require substantial floating-point operations (FLOPs) and high VRAM capacity, exhibiting latencies of 25–35 ms on high-end GPUs—nearly an order of magnitude slower than lightweight CNNs (3–5 ms) and excessively heavy for low-power edge gateways.
P[19] (Normal): Thus, industrial AOI confronts a fundamental architectural dilemma: lightweight CNNs offer the required frame rates but miss micro-scale defects, whereas Vision Transformers offer superior recall and global relational context but exceed edge hardware thermal and latency budgets.
P[20] (Normal): 1.5 Research Contributions
P[21] (Normal): To resolve this multi-scale, latency-constrained dilemma, this paper proposes an Adaptive Resolution-Aware Dual-Track Detection Framework specifically designed for high-precision, real-time PCB surface defect and foreign object debris inspection. The core contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
P[22] (Normal): 1. Lightweight Adaptive Resolution Router (ARR): We formulate a content-adaptive, complexity-aware preprocessing gate that dynamically determines whether an incoming PCB frame requires resolution enhancement prior to inference. By extracting spatial edge density, local contrast standard deviation, and high-frequency spectral energy via 2D Fast Fourier Transform, the ARR routes 55.13% of frames through a high-speed direct pathway and selectively enhances only the 44.87% exhibiting micro-defect characteristics. This eliminates computational redundancy, yielding an average latency reduction of 22.1 ms per frame (55.13% compute saving) over universal super-resolution baselines.
P[23] (Normal): 2. Asymmetric Dual-Track CNN-Transformer Architecture: We design an asymmetric dual-track detection engine that executes inference in parallel across two structurally complementary paradigms: Track 1 (Fast CNN: fine-tuned YOLO26n, 3.9 ms GPU latency, 2.57M parameters, 5.14 MB storage) for rapid localized defect extraction; and Track 2 (Contextual Transformer: fine-tuned RT-DETR-L, 28.0 ms GPU latency, 32M parameters) for long-range topological trace modeling.
P[24] (Normal): 3. Complementary Late Fusion via Weighted Box Fusion (WBF): Rather than relying on traditional greedy Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)—which discards redundant bounding boxes and ignores multi-model spatial agreement—we implement an asymmetric confidence-weighted box fusion scheme (w_DETR = 1.5, w_YOLO = 1.0). The fusion pipeline achieves an optimal balance between precision and recall, securing a peak F1-score of 0.6493 and eliminating 17 false positives present in standalone transformer predictions with zero recall degradation.
P[25] (Normal): 4. Edge-Constrained Quantization and Real-Time Benchmarking: We validate the deployability of our dual-track pipeline on constrained edge hardware via post-training dynamic INT8 quantization and structured pruning. The quantized models achieve a 3.41x compression ratio for YOLO26n (2.74 MB) and 3.87x for RT-DETR-L (32.45 MB), proving that dual-track fusion can be seamlessly hosted within the physical memory and latency constraints of industrial embedded PCs and edge accelerators.

P[30] (Normal): 2. Related Work / Literature Review
P[31] (Normal): 2.1 Deep Learning for PCB Automated Optical Inspection: The CNN vs. Transformer Evolution
P[32] (Normal): The development of modern deep-learning-based AOI systems has been largely catalyzed by successive iterations of the YOLO framework. Ye et al. [1] proposed an improved lightweight YOLOv11s model for PCB surface defect detection, incorporating channel and spatial attention mechanisms alongside structured parameter pruning to address edge memory limitations. Their system demonstrated high detection rates on standard benchmarks (DeepPCB and DsPCBSD+) while emphasizing the necessity of model compression for industrial shop-floor integration. Li et al. [2] introduced SCF-YOLO, a lightweight architecture utilizing a MobileNet-style backbone and feature-split shuffle blocks, combined with response-based knowledge distillation from a larger YOLOv8-l teacher network. While SCF-YOLO achieved a 25% parameter reduction and maintained high precision on the PKU-PCB dataset, its detection accuracy degraded noticeably on irregular particulate debris (such as FOD) due to the reduced representation capacity of depthwise separable convolutions.
P[33] (Normal): Concurrently, researchers have sought to enhance standard YOLO backbones with specialized attention mechanisms to counteract small-defect attenuation. Kong et al. [3] formulated GESC-YOLO, which integrates a C2f-GE feature extraction block, GSConv slim-neck layers, and Coordinate Attention (CA) into YOLOv8n, reporting up to 99% mAP@50 on balanced test sets. Chu et al. [4] investigated group convolutions and adaptive pruning via the LAMP algorithm, demonstrating that up to 35% of redundant convolutional weights in YOLOv8n could be removed with minimal accuracy loss on the PKU-Market-PCB dataset. In more recent explorations of next-generation backbones, Yin et al. [5] proposed MAS-YOLO, representing one of the earliest adaptations of YOLOv12 for PCB inspection, utilizing Multi-Scale Edge-Context Saliency (MECS) attention and an Asymmetric Heterogeneous Feature Interaction Neck (AHFIN). Selvam et al. [6] introduced YOLO-DefXpert, integrating Swin Transformer blocks, Deformable Convolutions (DCNv2), and the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) into YOLOv11 to improve defect boundaries. Chen and Liu [7] designed a high-accuracy YOLOv12 variant incorporating BiFPN and Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD) loss. Chen, Ke, and Meng [8] developed MS-YOLO (based on YOLOv10), integrating Multiscale Edge Strengthening (MSES) modules and Space-to-Depth Convolutions (SPDConv) alongside dual-distillation.
P[34] (Normal): Despite these convolutional advances, pure CNN architectures remain fundamentally bounded by localized receptive fields. When tracing extensive copper paths across a circuit board to detect discontinuous open circuits or subtle bridging, CNNs must stack dozens of layers to achieve sufficient contextual coverage, resulting in gradient degradation and feature blurring. To overcome this, Vision Transformers have been introduced into industrial inspection. Niaz et al. [20] proposed a hybrid architecture coupling Vision Transformers with Mamba state-space attention, achieving 99.69% mAP through selective state-space sequence modeling of PCB surface features. Niu et al. [23] developed LPDETR, a lightweight variant of the Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) that reduced parameter count by ~30% while retaining the global self-attention mechanism. Zhang et al. [28] designed MSA-DETR, incorporating multi-scale alignment feature pyramids to align spatial features across transformer decoding layers. However, all existing ViT and DETR implementations exhibit substantial computational latency (typically 25–45 ms), which remains an obstacle for continuous, high-speed AOI lines operating at >60 frames per second.
P[35] (Normal): 2.2 Micro-Defect Localization and Resolution Enhancement Strategies
P[36] (Normal): A critical vulnerability shared by both CNN and Transformer detectors is the severe performance drop encountered on micro-scale defects. On standard benchmarks such as DsPCBSD+, tiny defects like copper spurs (SP), fine conductor scratches (CS), and ball micro foreign objects (BMFO) occupy less than 0.5% of the total pixel area [2]. To restore lost spatial high-frequency details, researchers have investigated super-resolution (SR) networks as neural image enhancers. Modern deep SR frameworks—including SwinIR, Real-ESRGAN, and RCAN—employ deep residual networks or shifted-window self-attention to reconstruct sub-pixel edges, high-frequency textures, and sharp transition boundaries. In industrial domains, applying deep SR prior to detection has been shown to improve small-object mAP by 3.5% to 7.0%.
P[37] (Normal): However, deep super-resolution is computationally demanding. For instance, executing SwinIR-light on a 640 x 640 image consumes 50–120 ms on an embedded GPU—far exceeding the inference latency of the object detector itself. Critically, every existing study in the literature applies resolution enhancement uniformly across all inspection images [8, 14, 15]. In production environments, PCB images arrive in high-volume streams where over 50% of frames contain either macro-defects or standard conductor tracks that do not benefit from super-resolution. Applying deep SR indiscriminately to every image creates an unnecessary computational bottleneck. To date, no prior research has investigated an adaptive, content-aware routing gate that inspects low-level structural image complexity prior to detection to selectively trigger resolution enhancement only when microscopic anomalies are present.
P[38] (Normal): 2.3 Multi-Detector Ensembles and Late Fusion Techniques
P[39] (Normal): To combine the complementary strengths of different detection models, ensemble learning is widely employed in competitive computer vision. In PCB inspection, CNNs offer high localization speed and high precision on high-contrast edges, whereas Vision Transformers excel at contextual recall and resolving ambiguous, low-contrast traces. Traditionally, multi-detector predictions are merged using Greedy Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) or its variant, Soft-NMS. However, standard NMS possesses inherent structural flaws in multi-model settings: it assumes that the single bounding box with the highest confidence score represents the most accurate spatial ground truth, discarding all overlapping candidate boxes whose Intersection-over-Union (IoU) exceeds a threshold. If a detector incorrectly assigns high confidence to a false alarm, NMS preserves the error while discarding lower-confidence true positive predictions from secondary models.
P[40] (Normal): To overcome these deficiencies, Solovyev et al. introduced Weighted Box Fusion (WBF) [10]. Rather than discarding overlapping bounding boxes, WBF utilizes the confidence scores of all matching predictions across models to compute an averaged, confidence-weighted bounding box coordinate:
P[41] (Normal): While WBF has been utilized in aerial imagery and general object detection competitions, its application to industrial PCB defect detection—specifically as an asymmetric bridge between an ultra-lightweight CNN (YOLO) and a contextual hybrid transformer (RT-DETR)—remains largely unexplored.
P[42] (Normal): 2.4 Edge Model Compression, Pruning, and Quantization
P[43] (Normal): Deploying deep learning models on factory-floor Automated Optical Inspection equipment requires conformance to strict SWaP (Size, Weight, and Power) constraints. Production line cameras are typically connected to industrial personal computers (IPCs) or embedded accelerators (such as NVIDIA Jetson Orin/Nano or Intel OpenVINO edge gateways) possessing limited GPU VRAM (4GB–8GB) and strict thermal dissipation limits [1, 4, 23]. Model compression techniques primarily encompass structured pruning (e.g., L1-norm or LAMP algorithm [4]) and Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) from FP32 to FP16 and INT8. Quantization to INT8 reduces the model's memory footprint by up to 4x, lowers memory bandwidth pressure, and enables the utilization of specialized integer tensor cores. While individual papers have explored pruning on YOLOv8 [4] or knowledge distillation on YOLOv11 [1, 2], there is a distinct lack of comprehensive empirical research that benchmarks the end-to-end deployment pipeline—combining adaptive resolution routing, dual-track CNN-transformer inference, WBF late fusion, and full INT8 quantization—under uniform industrial edge constraints.

P[49] (Normal): 3.1 Dataset
P[50] (Normal): 3.1.1 Overview and Source Verification
P[51] (Normal): To evaluate the proposed framework under rigorous industrial conditions, we utilize the DsPCBSD+ (Deep-learning-based PCB Surface Defect Detection Dataset Plus) benchmark [21]. The dataset was established by Lv et al. and published in Scientific Data (Nature Portfolio, 2024, DOI: 10.1038/s41597-024-03657-z), released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License (CC BY 4.0) for open scientific benchmarking. DsPCBSD+ represents one of the largest and most comprehensive publicly available datasets dedicated to automated optical inspection of printed circuit boards. It comprises 10,259 high-resolution images, each captured at a standardized dimension of 640 x 640 pixels in 24-bit true color RGB format. The physical imaging was executed using an industrial telecentric optical inspection system equipped with a high-resolution CCD area scan camera and a coaxial LED illumination dome, specifically engineered to eliminate non-uniform specular reflections from bare copper traces and solder pads.
P[52] (Normal): 3.1.2 Defect Taxonomies and Morphological Characterization
P[53] (Normal): Unlike earlier PCB datasets (such as DeepPCB or PKU-PCB) that are restricted to six canonical etching defects, DsPCBSD+ provides exhaustive annotations across nine distinct industrial defect classes. Importantly, the dataset explicitly incorporates particulate contamination and Foreign Object Debris (FOD), which are critical failure modes in surface-mount production lines: (1) Short Circuit (SH), an unintended bridge of conductive material connecting isolated traces; (2) Spur (SP), a localized protrusion of excess copper extending outward from trace boundaries; (3) Spurious Copper (SC), isolated islands of unetched copper on dielectric substrates; (4) Open Circuit (OP), a complete physical fracture along a conductive trace; (5) Mask Break (MB), a void in the protective solder mask exposing underlying copper; (6) Hole Break (HB), an off-center or misaligned drill via fracturing through an annular ring; (7) Conductor Scratch (CS), mechanical abrasions thinning trace cross-sections; (8) Copper Foreign Object (CFO), extraneous conductive copper debris adhering to the board; and (9) Ball Micro Foreign Object (BMFO), microscopic spherical solder spatter or particulate debris.

P[92] (Normal): 3.3 YOLO26n Lightweight Object Detection
P[93] (Normal): YOLO26n was selected as the lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN)-based detection branch of the proposed PCB defect detection framework. The YOLO family is designed for object detection by directly predicting object locations and class probabilities from an input image, providing a computationally efficient alternative to multi-stage detection pipelines. The YOLO26 model family further incorporates an end-to-end detection design, a lightweight detection head, and an updated training strategy intended to improve the accuracy–latency trade-off for real-time vision applications.
P[94] (Normal): In the proposed framework, YOLO26n is used to provide a low-computational-cost detection pathway for identifying and localizing PCB defects. The model receives PCB images at a standardized resolution of 640 × 640 pixels and produces bounding-box coordinates, predicted defect classes, and confidence scores for detected regions. The lightweight model was selected to check whether efficient CNN-based detection can provide sufficiently accurate PCB inspection while maintaining a relatively small computational and memory footprint. The project documentation reports approximately 2.51 million parameters and a controlled GPU inference latency of approximately 3.9 ms for the YOLO26n track.
P[95] (Normal): The YOLO26n branch is particularly relevant to PCB inspection because several defects occupy relatively small regions of the image. Consequently, the detector must simultaneously preserve fine spatial information and maintain efficient inference. The use of a lightweight detector provides a baseline against which the computationally larger transformer-based RT-DETR-L model can be evaluated. Rather than assuming that one detector architecture is universally optimal, the two models are evaluated as complementary detection pathways with different computational and representational characteristics. This model-selection objective is consistent with the project's stated goal of balancing detection accuracy, localization quality, inference speed, and model size.
P[96] (Normal): For each input image, the YOLO26n model generates a set of candidate detections represented by their bounding-box coordinates, class labels, and confidence scores. Predictions below the configured confidence threshold are discarded before subsequent visualization and fusion. The remaining detections constitute the YOLO26n prediction set and are subsequently made available to the late-fusion stage.
P[97] (Normal): 3.4 RT-DETR-L Transformer-Based Object Detection
P[98] (Normal): RT-DETR-L was incorporated as the transformer-based detection branch of the proposed framework to provide a complementary representation to the lightweight YOLO26n detector. Real-Time DEtection TRansformer (RT-DETR) is an end-to-end transformer-based object detector derived from the DETR family. It uses an efficient hybrid encoder for multi-scale feature processing and a transformer decoder for object prediction, while avoiding the conventional non-maximum suppression stage used by many conventional object-detection pipelines.
P[99] (Normal): The principal motivation for incorporating RT-DETR-L is to evaluate whether a detector with stronger global contextual modelling can identify PCB defects that may be difficult to characterize using only local convolutional features. PCB inspection involves structured visual patterns such as conductive traces, gaps, holes, scratches, and foreign objects. The spatial relationship between a defect and surrounding PCB structures can therefore provide useful contextual information during detection. RT-DETR provides an alternative detection paradigm in which object queries are used to generate a fixed set of predictions from the encoded image representation.
P[100] (Normal): Within the proposed framework, RT-DETR-L processes the same preprocessed PCB image as the YOLO26n branch. The model produces bounding boxes, defect categories, and confidence values that form an independent prediction set. The project implementation uses the RT-DETR-L variant with approximately 32.82 million parameters and a controlled GPU inference latency of approximately 28.0 ms, substantially exceeding the computational requirements of the YOLO26n branch.
P[101] (Normal): The difference between the two detectors is also reflected in their observed detection characteristics. In the project analysis, YOLO26n produced a precision of 0.6340, recall of 0.6578, and F1-score of 0.6457, whereas RT-DETR-L produced a precision of 0.4924, recall of 0.8663, and F1-score of 0.6279. Thus, the transformer branch demonstrated substantially higher recall while also generating a larger number of false-positive detections. This complementary behaviour provides the motivation for combining the two prediction sets using Weighted Box Fusion rather than relying exclusively on either detector.
P[102] (Normal): Accordingly, RT-DETR-L is not treated simply as a replacement for YOLO26n. Instead, it constitutes the second prediction pathway in a dual-track framework in which computational efficiency and contextual detection characteristics are evaluated jointly. Its predictions are passed to the late-fusion stage together with YOLO26n predictions, where spatially compatible detections can be combined.
P[103] (Normal): 3.5 Adaptive Resolution Routing
P[104] (Normal): High-resolution processing can improve the visibility of small and fine-grained PCB defects, but applying computationally expensive super-resolution processing to every input image introduces unnecessary computational overhead when the original image already contains sufficient structural information. To address this trade-off, the proposed framework incorporates a defect-aware adaptive resolution router that determines the preprocessing pathway for each PCB image according to measurable image characteristics.
P[105] (Normal): The routing mechanism evaluates three image-level characteristics: Canny edge density, contrast variance, and the high-frequency energy ratio obtained from the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Canny edge density provides an estimate of the amount of structural edge information present in the image, while contrast variance characterizes intensity variation. The FFT-based high-frequency energy ratio provides a frequency-domain descriptor of fine image details. These characteristics are jointly used by the decision mechanism to determine whether additional resolution enhancement is required.
P[106] (Normal): The first pathway performs direct resizing to 640 × 640 pixels and forwards the resulting image to the detection models. The second pathway applies 2× super-resolution using SwinIR-light before the detection stage. SwinIR is a transformer-based image-restoration architecture designed for tasks including image super-resolution, denoising, and compression-artifact reduction. Its architecture combines shallow feature extraction, deep feature extraction using residual Swin Transformer blocks, and high-quality image reconstruction.
P[107] (Normal): The purpose of using super-resolution selectively rather than universally is to reduce unnecessary preprocessing overhead. Images containing sufficient structural and high-frequency information can therefore bypass the additional enhancement stage, while images identified as requiring additional detail reconstruction are routed through the super-resolution pathway. The project documentation reports that 55.13% of the benchmark images were processed through the direct 640 × 640 pathway, while 44.87% were routed through the super-resolution pathway, resulting in a reported average computational saving of 22.1 ms per image.
P[108] (Normal): The adaptive router therefore operates as a preprocessing decision layer rather than as a detector itself. Its output determines the representation supplied to the YOLO26n and RT-DETR-L branches, allowing the subsequent detectors to operate using a resolution selected according to the characteristics of the individual input image. The routing distribution and measured preprocessing saving indicate that selective resolution enhancement can reduce the computational burden associated with applying super-resolution indiscriminately.
P[109] (Normal): 3.6 Weighted Box Fusion
P[110] (Normal): The proposed framework combines the predictions generated independently by YOLO26n and RT-DETR-L using Weighted Box Fusion (WBF). WBF was selected as the late-fusion mechanism because the two detectors exhibit complementary prediction characteristics: YOLO26n provides a lightweight and spatially precise prediction pathway, whereas RT-DETR-L provides higher recall through its stronger contextual representation. Rather than selecting predictions from a single detector, WBF combines spatially corresponding detections from both models into a refined consensus prediction.
P[111] (Normal): For each input image, the two detection branches generate bounding boxes represented by their coordinates, predicted class labels, and confidence scores. Before fusion, predictions below the configured confidence threshold are removed. Bounding boxes belonging to the same defect class are then compared according to their Intersection-over-Union (IoU). Predictions whose spatial overlap exceeds the predefined fusion threshold are considered to represent the same underlying defect instance.
P[112] (Normal): For a group of N matching bounding boxes, the fused bounding-box coordinates are calculated using confidence-weighted averaging. For the x-coordinate, for example, the fused coordinate can be expressed as
P[113] (Normal): x_f = [Σ(i=1 to N) w_i c_i x_i] / [Σ(i=1 to N) w_i c_i]
P[114] (Normal): where x_i represents the corresponding coordinate of the i-th prediction, c_i is its confidence score, and w_i is the model-specific fusion weight. The same formulation is applied independently to the remaining bounding-box coordinates. The resulting fused confidence is derived from the confidence contributions of the participating predictions.
P[115] (Normal): In the proposed implementation, an asymmetric weighting strategy is used to reflect the complementary characteristics of the two detectors. Predictions from YOLO26n are assigned a weight of 1.0, while RT-DETR-L predictions are assigned a higher weight of 1.5. The higher RT-DETR-L weight reflects its stronger contextual detection behaviour and higher observed recall in the validation experiments. An IoU threshold of 0.5 is used to determine whether predictions from the two models correspond to the same defect instance.
P[116] (Normal): The use of WBF differs from conventional Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS). NMS retains the highest-confidence prediction and suppresses overlapping boxes, whereas WBF preserves information from multiple agreeing predictions and uses their spatial and confidence information to obtain a consolidated bounding box. This is particularly relevant to the proposed dual-track architecture because agreement between the CNN and transformer branches can provide additional evidence for a defect while reducing duplicate or isolated false detections. The final fused output consists of the defect class, refined bounding-box coordinates, and associated confidence score, and is subsequently passed to the evaluation and visualization stages.
P[117] (Normal): The WBF stage therefore serves as the late-fusion layer between the two independent detection paradigms. It does not introduce additional trainable parameters or require retraining of either detector; instead, it operates directly on the prediction sets produced by YOLO26n and RT-DETR-L. The resulting ensemble is evaluated against the individual detectors using precision, recall, F1-score, and false-positive counts in Section 4.
P[118] (Normal): 3.7 Model Compression
P[119] (Normal): To improve the suitability of the detection framework for resource-constrained deployment, model compression was investigated after training the YOLO26n and RT-DETR-L detectors. The compression stage consists of L1-based unstructured pruning followed by post-training numerical quantization, with the objective of reducing model storage and computational resource requirements while retaining the detection capability of the original models. The use of pruning and quantization is motivated by the limited memory, compute capacity, and power budget typically associated with edge-based inspection systems. The literature reviewed for this work similarly identifies pruning and quantization as important approaches for reducing the deployment cost of PCB defect detection models.
P[120] (Normal): 3.7.1 L1-Unstructured Pruning
P[121] (Normal): The first compression stage applies L1-unstructured pruning to the trained detector parameters. In unstructured pruning, individual weights with relatively small absolute magnitude are identified and set to zero rather than removing complete filters, channels, or network layers. The L1 criterion ranks parameters according to their absolute magnitude:
P[122] (Normal): s(w_i) = |w_i|
P[123] (Normal): where w_i denotes an individual model weight and s(w_i) represents its L1 importance score. Parameters with the lowest importance scores are selected for removal according to the predefined sparsity level.
P[124] (Normal): A target sparsity of 30% is used in the current implementation. Thus, approximately 30% of the selected weights are converted to zero while retaining the original network topology. This approach reduces parameter redundancy without requiring a complete architectural redesign of either detector. The Review-2 implementation records L1-unstructured pruning as part of the model-compression pipeline.
P[125] (Normal): Following pruning, the models are evaluated to determine whether the introduced sparsity causes a measurable change in detection performance. Precision, recall, F1-score, and other detection metrics are compared with the corresponding unpruned models. The quantitative effect of pruning is reported separately in Section 4.4.
P[126] (Normal): 3.7.2 Post-Training Quantization
P[127] (Normal): Following the pruning stage, the trained models are exported to the Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX) format to facilitate hardware-independent inference and subsequent optimization. Post-training quantization is then applied to reduce the numerical precision of model parameters from 32-bit floating-point representation to lower-precision formats.
P[128] (Normal): The primary deployment configuration uses dynamic INT8 quantization. In this process, model weights are represented using 8-bit integer values rather than FP32 values. For a floating-point value x, the corresponding quantized representation can be expressed conceptually as
P[129] (Normal): q = round(x / s) + z
P[130] (Normal): where q is the quantized integer value, s is the scale factor, and z is the zero-point. During dynamic quantization, the required scaling parameters are determined during inference rather than requiring a separate calibration dataset. This allows the trained models to be compressed without retraining the detection networks.
P[131] (Normal): The compression procedure is applied independently to YOLO26n and RT-DETR-L so that the effect of reduced numerical precision can be evaluated for both detector architectures. The resulting INT8 models retain the original detection interfaces while requiring substantially less storage than their corresponding floating-point ONNX representations. The current implementation reports compressed model sizes of 2.74 MB for YOLO26n and 32.45 MB for RT-DETR-L, corresponding to approximately 3.41× and 3.87× reductions, respectively, relative to their ONNX FP32 representations.
P[132] (Normal): The compressed models are subsequently used for CPU-oriented benchmarking to examine the practical relationship between model footprint and inference latency. In addition to storage reduction, this evaluation is intended to determine whether the compressed models satisfy the project's edge-deployment requirements. The detailed effects of pruning and INT8 quantization on model size, detection performance, latency, and throughput are presented in Sections 4.4 and 4.5, while the overall computational and deployment comparison is discussed in Section 4.6.
